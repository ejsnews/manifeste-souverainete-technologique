# Chapter 5 — Biochar Territories: Towards a Local Circular Economy

> Version 1.0 – July 2026

---

# Abstract

Biochar is often presented merely as an agricultural soil amendment.

This view is reductive.

When produced locally from sustainable biomass, it can serve as a meeting point for several public policy areas:

- agriculture;
- energy;
- organic waste management;
- industry;
- water;
- climate;
- territorial sovereignty.

This chapter proposes the concept of the **Biochar Territory**, a forward-looking circular economy model based on the local valorization of biomass.

---

# Why a territory?

Biomass is a diffuse resource.

Transporting it over long distances often diminishes its economic and environmental value.

Conversely, local valorization limits material flows and creates synergies among local stakeholders.

---

# Available resources

Depending on the region, several resource streams can be utilized:

| Resource | Examples |
|-----------|----------|
| Agricultural residues | straw, stalks, cobs/rachises |
| Forestry residues | branches, thinning residues |
| Bamboo | dedicated plantations or residues |
| Green waste | trimmings, prunings |
| Industrial co-products | sawdust, wood chips, husks/shells |

The choice of feedstocks must remain compatible with sustainable resource management.

---

# Stakeholders in a Biochar Territory

A territory can bring together:

- farmers;
- local authorities;
- forestry operators;
- waste management companies;
- industrial players;
- research centers;
- educational institutions;
- citizens.

Cooperation among these stakeholders forms the foundation of the model. ---

# A multifunctional platform

A thermolysis unit can simultaneously produce:

- biochar;
- useful heat;
- syngas;
- renewable fuels, depending on the technology used;
- renewable hydrogen, via certain gasification or reforming pathways.

The exact combination depends on the industrial process.

---

# Benefits for agriculture

Biochar can help to:

- improve certain soils;
- limit nutrient leaching;
- increase resilience to drought;
- foster microbial life when combined with organic matter.

Results always depend on local conditions.

---

# Benefits for industry

Local businesses can use biochar in:

- construction materials;
- composites;
- filters;
- certain metallurgical applications;
- carbon-based materials.

Developing new uses fosters economic diversification.

---

# A climate strategy

The "Biochar Territory" approach can help to:

- valorize underutilized residues;
- reduce emissions associated with open-air burning;
- durably store a portion of carbon in a stable form;
- produce renewable energy, depending on the chosen processes.

These benefits must be assessed on a case-by-case basis.

---

# Jobs

A local value chain can generate jobs in:

- biomass collection;
- logistics;
- facility operations;
- laboratories;
- maintenance;
- engineering and design firms;
- research.

Job creation naturally depends on project scale and the economic context. ---

# Digital Data

Each territory could publish:

- available biomass;
- production volumes;
- estimated avoided emissions;
- restored land areas;
- biochar applications.

This data would feed into the Global Biochar Observatory.

---

# Limitations

The success of a "Biochar Territory" requires:

- sustainably available biomass;
- local governance;
- economic viability;
- social acceptance;
- continuous environmental assessment.

Consequently, the model cannot be replicated identically everywhere.

---

# A Forward-Looking Vision

In the long term, thousands of Biochar Territories could form an international network for sharing experiences.

Each territory would retain its unique characteristics while sharing:

- its data;
- its best practices;
- its innovations.

Such an approach would foster continuous process improvement.

---

# Conclusion

Biochar can be viewed not merely as a product, but as a catalyst for territorial cooperation.

The **Biochar Territory** concept offers a vision in which agriculture, energy, industry, climate, and resource management converge toward a more circular and resilient economy.

It is a forward-looking proposal intended to inform the discussion on local development models.
